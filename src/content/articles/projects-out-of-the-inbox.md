Small teams already have a project management system. It's the inbox, a few pinned chats, a notepad by the keyboard and whoever remembers best. For a long time that system is genuinely good. It costs nothing, needs no setup, and everyone already knows how to use it.

Project tools get sold as if the inbox were a problem to be solved. Usually it isn't, at least not yet. The better question is whether the work has changed shape since you started.

## The work usually changes before the tools do

The inbox copes well with work that is **short, sequential and owned by one person**. You get a request, you do it, you reply. It copes badly with three things, and they tend to arrive together as a business grows:

- **Work that waits on someone else.** When a task is blocked by a client, a supplier or a colleague, the inbox has no way to show that it is blocked. It just sits there looking like it's yours.
- **Work with more than one owner.** "Who's doing this?" is the most expensive question in a small team, because the honest answer is often "we both assumed the other one was."
- **Work that runs longer than a week.** Anything with stages, like a launch, a client onboarding or a website rebuild, needs to be seen whole. An inbox only shows you the most recent message about it.

If most of your work is still the first kind, a project tool will feel like admin. If it has drifted towards the other three, the inbox is now hiding things from you.

## A quick check

Try answering these without opening anything:

1. What is every person on the team working on this week?
2. Which tasks are waiting on someone outside the business?
3. What is due in the next fourteen days?

If you can answer all three, you probably don't need a tool yet. If you'd have to search your email, scroll a chat and ask around, that searching is the real cost of the current system, and it grows with every new client.

## Choose by upkeep, not by features

Every tool on our [project management shortlist](/project-management-software) can hold a task, a due date and an owner. What separates them is how much maintenance each one asks of you, and that is what decides whether the tool is still in use in three months.

- **[Notion](/notion-review)** is a blank page you shape yourself. That's ideal if someone on the team enjoys building a system, and a trap if nobody does.
- **[ClickUp](/clickup-review)** gives you almost everything out of the box and expects you to switch off what you don't need. It's powerful, and it rewards someone willing to prune it.
- **[Asana](/asana-review)** decides more of the structure for you. There's less to configure and less to argue about, which is often exactly what a busy team needs.

Pick the one whose upkeep matches the least organised person who will have to use it. A tool fails when the busiest person on the team stops opening it, and after that everyone else stops too.

## Moving in without the big-bang migration

Don't copy every open email thread into the new tool on day one. Start with **one real project that is already running**, add only the tasks still outstanding, and give each one an owner and a date. Run it there for two weeks. If the team keeps opening the tool without being reminded, add the next project. If they don't, you've learned that cheaply, and the inbox is still right where you left it.
