Asana is our default recommendation for a small team that needs clear owners, due dates, and dependencies without a long build phase. The other picks earn their place when the job changes: ClickUp for consolidating several tools into one workspace, Trello for a visual board workflow, monday Work Management for structured operating boards, Notion for work that lives in docs and databases, and Teamwork.com for client work that has to become an invoice.

We checked the public pricing and plan pages on October 18, 2026, and we’ve kept the plan gates beside the recommendations because those gates are where a cheap project tool usually gets expensive.

## What the plan gates actually decide

Before the product notes, it helps to separate three buying questions that roundups tend to blur.

**Seats are not the same as collaborators.** Some tools bill every internal member in a workspace. Some let free guests view but not edit. Some treat guests who sit on multiple boards as billable users. That matters when a client, bookkeeper, or fractional teammate needs access but shouldn’t cost a full seat.

**The view you want is usually a plan gate.** Timeline or Gantt views, reports, workload views, forms, and automation limits routinely sit one or two tiers above the free plan. A tool can look cheap at the headline and stop fitting exactly where your team begins depending on it.

**AI is metered differently in each product.** Asana includes a small request pool and sells more. Notion prices Custom Agents by credits. Teamwork.com plans usage-based AI credits. ClickUp includes a monthly credit allowance with plan-specific premium model access, then sells extra credits. If an AI assistant becomes part of a recurring workflow, price that usage separately.

## 1. Asana: best for accountability and dependencies

Asana is the pick when the failure you’re trying to prevent is a task falling between people. A task belongs to one assignee, carries a due date, and can be blocked by another task. That is a simple model, and it’s exactly why Asana holds up as work becomes more interdependent.

On [Asana’s pricing page](https://asana.com/pricing), the free Personal plan is now for up to 2 users, not the old 10- or 15-user free tier you’ll still see repeated. It includes unlimited tasks and projects, list, board, and calendar views, status updates, and file storage with a 100 MB per-file limit.

The paid Starter plan, listed at $10.99 per user per month billed yearly, unlocks the features most small teams actually need: timeline and Gantt views, custom fields, forms, reporting dashboards, custom templates, unlimited automations, and unlimited free guests. Advanced, at $24.99 per user per month billed yearly, adds portfolios, goals, approvals and proofing, resource management, and native time tracking.

For a 5-person team, Starter works out to $659.40 for the first year on annual billing. The same 5 seats on Advanced works out to $1,499.40. Asana’s published seat-add rules say you can add seats in increments of one between 2 and 5 total users, so a five-seat purchase is a valid configuration—but still confirm the checkout total.

AI is the trap to price deliberately. Asana includes a small pool of AI requests per plan, then sells additional requests at $0.50 each prepaid or $0.60 each on demand, according to the pricing page we checked. If your team leans on AI summaries or AI Teammates, those charges sit on top of the seat price.

Choose Asana when you want to inherit a process and keep handoffs visible. Skip it when you need native client budgets and invoices as the reason you’re buying software in the first place.

## 2. ClickUp: best for replacing several tools

ClickUp earns its spot when you’re trying to collapse tasks, docs, goals, time tracking, chat, and project views into one login. For a small team with a collection of disconnected apps, that coverage is a real reason to choose it.

The [current public pricing page](https://clickup.com/pricing) we checked on October 18, 2026 shows a new structure: Free Forever, Core, Business, Enterprise, and MAX. Free Forever still gives you unlimited members and tasks, collaborative docs, Kanban and calendar views, sprint management, time tracking, and basic support, but it carries real limits: 60 MB of storage, one form, and feature usage caps. On the free plan, guests have full editing access that cannot be restricted.

Core, displayed at $9 per user per month billed yearly with a struck-through regular price of $12, removes the sharpest free-plan limits. It adds unlimited storage, spaces, forms, Gantt charts, and chat, plus guest permissions, 1,000 automations per month, and a 1,000 pooled AI credit allowance per user per month. Business, displayed at $17 per user per month billed yearly with a struck-through regular price of $25, adds advanced dashboards, resource management, private views and docs, timesheets, 10,000 automations per month, and 1,500 pooled AI credits per user per month with premium model access.

At the displayed annual offers, five paid users on Core works out to $540 for a year. Five paid users on Business works out to $1,020. On the monthly-billing toggle, Core displayed $12 per user and Business $25 per user, which would total $720 and $1,500 respectively across a year. We could not verify the renewal rate after the displayed offer, so confirm what you’ll pay once the first term ends.

ClickUp bills every paid member in the workspace. Internal employees are paid users, not free guests. Paid plans include limited editable guest seats and unlimited read-only guests, but verify the exact allowance before inviting several outside editors.

AI is included with a plan-specific credit allowance rather than sold as a blanket add-on. Extra AI Super Credits cost $10 per 10,000 credits, and specialized services like Certified Agents and ClickUp Assist are separate purchases.

Choose ClickUp when you can name the person who will own the configuration and prune the defaults. Skip it when that person doesn’t exist. The coverage is real, but it asks for discipline.

## 3. Trello: best for visual small-team flow

Trello is the strongest pick here when the board is the entire process. Cards move left to right, and a new team member can read the setup without a manual.

The [Trello plan comparison](https://trello.com/pricing) lists a free plan with up to 10 collaborators per Workspace, up to 10 boards, unlimited cards, unlimited Power-Ups per board, and 250 Workspace automation command runs per month. File uploads are limited to 10 MB per file.

Standard, at $5 per user per month billed yearly, removes the board limit, raises file uploads to 250 MB per file, and adds advanced checklists, custom fields, list colors, saved searches, single-board guests, and 1,000 automation runs per month.

Premium, at $10 per user per month billed yearly, adds the views that help beyond a single board: calendar, timeline, table, dashboard, map, and workspace-level table and calendar views. It also includes unlimited automation runs and more admin controls.

For a 5-person team, Standard is $300 per year on annual billing, or $360 if you pay monthly across the year. Premium is $600 annually, or $750 across the year on monthly billing.

The billing nuance is multi-board guests. A single-board guest is different from a guest who sits on more than one board in a Workspace; multi-board guests are billable like regular members. If a contractor or client needs to see several boards, that changes your cost.

Choose Trello when the whole job maps cleanly to a board. Skip it when work has meaningful dependencies, several linked projects, or resource and budget tracking. Those needs push you toward Asana, ClickUp, or monday.

## 4. monday.com Work Management: best for structured operating boards

The naming distinction matters: this is monday Work Management, not monday CRM and not monday dev. These are separate products under the same brand, and their plan names and limits do not transfer.

monday Work Management turns boards, columns, and items into organized operating views. It is more structured than a blank database and more visual than a long task list, which makes it a strong fit for a business that wants repeatable views of clients, projects, launches, or recurring processes.

On the [monday Work Management pricing page](https://monday.com/pricing), the free plan supports 2 seats, up to 3 boards, up to 3 docs, and a limited set of column types. With a 10-seat selection, the page showed Basic at $9 per seat per month billed yearly, Standard at $12, and Pro at $19. monday’s own FAQ says paid plans start from 3 users, so small-team seat packages need a quote rather than an arbitrary multiplication from the headline rate.

Basic adds unlimited free viewers and unlimited items, but the capabilities a growing team usually needs live on Standard: timeline and Gantt views, calendar view, guest access, 250 automation actions and 250 integration actions per month. Pro expands automations and integrations to 25,000 actions each per month and adds private boards and docs, time tracking, and advanced columns.

For the captured 10-seat scenario, Standard works out to $1,440 per year before current AI credit offers, add-ons, or seat-package terms. The automation and integration action limits are the quiet constraint on Standard. A team that connects monday to several other apps can consume those actions faster than the price suggests.

Choose monday Work Management when structured operating boards solve the problem. Skip it when documents and linked databases are the primary working surface, or when you need native budgets, retainers, and client invoices.

## 5. Notion: best for docs and work context

Notion is not a project management tool in the same sense as Asana or monday. It is a workspace where pages, databases, and linked views can become a project system if someone builds one.

That distinction is the whole decision. A brief, a task database, meeting notes, and a client wiki can live in one Notion workspace and reference each other. For knowledge-heavy small teams, that removes the friction of a task tool and a doc tool drifting apart.

On [Notion’s pricing page](https://www.notion.com/pricing), the free plan gives individuals unlimited blocks, while workspaces with multiple members face a block limit. Databases include subtasks, dependencies, custom properties, and filtering. File uploads are capped at 5 MB, page history is 7 days, and external guests are limited to 10.

Notion displayed Plus at $10 per member per month and Business at $20 per member per month on October 18, 2026. We could not fully confirm the rendered billing toggle or term on that page, so treat those as displayed rates and verify annual versus monthly billing and currency before buying.

Plus removes the team block limit, removes the 5 MB upload cap, extends page history to 30 days, and adds custom forms and sites. Business is where stronger controls arrive: SAML SSO, granular database permissions, private teamspaces, AI Meeting Notes, Notion Agent, and Enterprise Search.

The AI boundary matters. Business AI features are not unlimited AI. Notion describes Custom Agents as free to try, then priced using Notion credits at $10 per 1,000 monthly credits. If you plan to deploy agents across many workflows, you need a usage estimate rather than just a seat estimate.

Choose Notion when one person will design the structure and your work needs documentation as much as task tracking. Skip it when you need native scheduling, billable time, resource allocation, or budgets.

## 6. Teamwork.com: best for client work that has to become an invoice

Teamwork.com is the pick for a small agency, consultancy, or services team whose project tool has to answer a second question: did this project make money?

The free plan is bounded but useful for a trial: up to 5 users, up to 5 projects, task, list, board, Gantt, and table views, 100 MB of storage, and 100 automations. It lets you log time and organize work by client before paying.

On [Teamwork.com’s pricing page](https://www.teamwork.com/pricing/), Basics and Accelerate are displayed with a corrupted currency symbol in our capture, so do not treat the numbers as verified US dollars. The pricing comparison table does state that Basics has a minimum of 3 seats and Accelerate a minimum of 5 seats. Basics includes Gantt, table, list, and board views plus billable time tracking.

Accelerate is where Teamwork.com separates itself for client teams. It adds routed work requests through smart forms, 20,000 automations, team workload and capacity views, time budgets and retainers, client invoices built from logged time, and HubSpot and QuickBooks connections. If you need to turn time into an invoice without exporting to a spreadsheet, that sequence is the reason to consider this product.

Confirm the actual currency, annual-billing total, and seat packaging with Teamwork.com before comparing it against a US-dollar-priced competitor.

Teamwork.com notes that AI features are included during an introductory period and that usage-based AI credits were planned to launch for paid plans. We would not build a long-term budget on AI remaining free forever.

Choose Teamwork.com when client billing, budgets, retainer tracking, and capacity planning are part of the weekly routine. Skip it if you’re mainly coordinating internal work and don’t need the financial layer.

## Run a small pilot before you pay

A seven-day pilot should test the handoff, dependency, guest-permission, entitlement, and export paths. We’re not claiming we ran these account tasks for you; these are the checks to perform inside a vendor trial, public sandbox, or authorized test role.

1. **Import a fictional sample.** Bring in a small list of tasks with owners, dates, and comments.
2. **Create the core work item.** Task, card, item, database row, or project—whatever the tool calls it.
3. **Test the handoff.** Assign it to someone, change the status, and confirm the next person sees it.
4. **Add a dependency.** Block one piece of work on another and watch what the tool communicates.
5. **Add a test guest or test role.** Check whether read-only, editable, or billable behavior matches what you’d want for a real client or contractor.
6. **Compare trial entitlements with your intended paid plan.** Confirm that what you tested matches what you’d actually buy, and note the features that disappear on downgrade.
7. **Export the result.** Confirm what comes back out: tasks, comments, files, dependencies, and links.

## Stay or switch: a small migration checklist

Before you move from your current tool, decide whether the missing requirement is worth the rebuild. Staying put is a legitimate answer when the current tool still covers the core job and the new pick mainly adds cost.

If you do switch, map these things before the cutover:

- **Owners and assignees.** Confirm the new tool imports task owners without reassigning everything to the admin account.
- **Dependencies and dates.** Check what happens to blocked items, relative dates, and recurring tasks.
- **Guest permissions.** Decide which clients or contractors need read-only access and whether each guest is billable.
- **Attachments and comments.** Test a small import to see whether files and discussion history survive.
- **Notifications.** Rebuild the minimum notification set before inviting the full team so the tool isn’t shouting on day one.
- **Reversible cutover.** Keep the old tool readable for at least one billing cycle and run a final export with an audit trail.

These are checks to perform in a sandbox or small import, not outcomes we measured. If the import loses something material, that’s a cost to weigh against the new tool’s value, not a bug to ignore.

## Next step

Start with the product that matches the job your team will do every week, not the longest feature list. If ownership and due dates are the problem, try Asana. If you’re replacing several tools, try ClickUp. If the board is the whole process, try Trello. If client time and budgets drive your profit, get a Teamwork.com quote before comparing it with anything else.

Then run one real project for a week, with the smallest useful set of views and notifications turned on. See our broader [project management buying guide](/project-management-software) for related comparisons and reviews. The tool that survives that week without becoming someone’s second job is the one worth paying for.
